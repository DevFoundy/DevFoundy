Chess, Video Games, and the Art of Thinking Ahead

How chess, video games, and early attempts at game development shaped the way I think about patience, creativity, and enjoying the process.

By KentaNovember 6, 20265 min read

DevFoundry Founder Journal | November 2026

How I Accidentally Got Into Chess

I think the way I discovered chess is actually pretty funny.

When I was around eight years old, I was really into video games. Like many kids, I had screen-time restrictions set by my parents, meaning I only had a limited amount of time to play games on my devices.

Somehow, I ended up discovering chess on the App Store.

At the time, I had absolutely no idea what I was doing. I knew how the pieces moved, but that was pretty much it. I would make random moves without thinking about strategies, openings, or what my opponent might do next.

To me, chess was simply another game.

It wasn't until late 2024 that I started taking it more seriously.

Getting Destroyed by My Teacher

Around that time, I noticed two of my favourite computing teachers playing chess.

Naturally, I wanted to try playing against one of them.

That didn't go particularly well.

I lost almost instantly. It turned out that my teacher had experience competing in chess tournaments, while I barely understood basic strategy.

I actually found the whole experience pretty funny. Looking back, I was essentially challenging someone with competitive experience while making moves based on whatever seemed reasonable at the time.

But that experience made me interested in learning more.

I started studying basic chess strategies, trying to understand why certain moves worked and others didn't.

A few months later, I entered my first chess tournament.

My final result? Two wins and four losses.

Not exactly an incredible tournament performance, but I had gone from randomly moving pieces around to actually competing against other players.

And I think that's worth something.

What Chess Has Taught Me

One of the biggest things chess has taught me is patience.

When you're playing, it's tempting to make the first move that looks good. But sometimes, taking a little longer to think can make a huge difference.

I've also learned the importance of staying calm.

I like to think of it as maintaining a poker face. Even when things aren't going particularly well, there's no reason to panic or immediately show frustration.

Of course, knowing that you should stay calm and actually doing it are two different things.

But I think chess is a good reminder that you don't always need to react immediately. Sometimes, the better decision comes from giving yourself enough time to think.

And that idea isn't limited to chess.

Video Games Are Also About Having Fun

Outside chess, I've always enjoyed video games.

Nowadays, I particularly enjoy first-person shooters and driving games. I also like exploring indie games, partly because I'm curious about the creative ideas behind them.

But I don't want to pretend that every time I play a game, I'm analyzing its mechanics or thinking about how the developers programmed it.

Most of the time, I'm just having fun.

For me, gaming is an opportunity to disconnect from everything else for a while. Whether it's school, programming, or other responsibilities, sometimes I simply want to spend time doing something enjoyable.

I think that's perfectly reasonable.

Not every hobby needs to become productive. Not everything we do needs to teach us a valuable life lesson.

Sometimes, entertainment can just be entertainment.

At the same time, I still find it fascinating that someone can come up with an idea, turn it into a playable game, and make the experience genuinely enjoyable for other people.

The Indie Developer Who Inspired Me

One game developer whose work I particularly enjoy is Dani, also known as Danidev on YouTube.

What I like about his videos and games is how simple and entertaining they can be.

I currently own two of his games, and although he hasn't been regularly uploading videos, I still appreciate his approach to development.

His work reminds me that an idea doesn't necessarily need to be extremely complicated to become something enjoyable.

Sometimes, a relatively simple concept, executed well, can create a memorable experience.

That's something I find interesting as someone who enjoys building software.

My Own Attempts at Game Development

My interest in game development actually started before I seriously got into Python or traditional programming.

When I was younger, I created quite a few games using Scratch.

Eventually, I became interested in more advanced game development tools and decided to experiment with Blender and Unity.

Unfortunately, I quickly discovered that these programs were much more complicated than I expected.

There were so many things to understand, and at the time, I didn't have the programming knowledge or experience to make much progress.

Eventually, I stopped trying.

But I don't really see that as the end of my interest in game development.

Nowadays, I'm much more focused on software and website development. I enjoy creating applications, experimenting with interfaces, and keeping up with current web design trends.

I often look at what other developers and designers are doing and consider how I could incorporate interesting ideas into my own projects.

Still, I hope to return to game development someday, once I've developed stronger programming skills.

After all, making games was one of the first ways I experimented with bringing my ideas to life.

You Don't Have to Be Good at Something to Enjoy It

If there's one thing I believe about chess, gaming, and programming, it's that you don't need to be particularly good at something to enjoy doing it.

When I first played chess, I barely understood what I was doing.

When I first experimented with Unity and Blender, I found them too complicated.

And when I started exploring programming, there was still an enormous amount I didn't understand.

But I think people sometimes put too much pressure on themselves to improve as quickly as possible.

My perspective is a little different.

If you genuinely enjoy something and keep doing it regularly, you'll probably improve over time without constantly forcing yourself to get better.

That doesn't mean practice or effort doesn't matter. It simply means that enjoyment can be one of the strongest reasons to keep going.

You don't need to win every chess game, become the best player in a video game, or build an incredible application on your first attempt.

Sometimes, it's enough to enjoy the process.

And if you keep coming back to something because you genuinely like it, you might be surprised by how far you eventually progress.

Written from my personal experiences and perspectives as the founder of DevFoundry.
