Inside Paper I: Attachment, Attraction, and the Questions Between Them

How I’m organising the first paper around five distinct concepts, five hypotheses, and explanations that still need testing.

Intouch Kenta TakizawaNovember 13, 20263 min read

The hardest part of writing this paper has been making sure similar words do not quietly become the same idea. Loneliness, emotional attachment, and romantic attraction can sound connected, but my project needs to leave room for them to describe different experiences.

Paper I is a theoretical literature review and a framework for a later study. Its purpose is to organise the question and explain what I want to test. There are no original survey findings in this first paper.

Five concepts I’m keeping separate

Loneliness concerns whether someone feels their social connections meet their needs. Anthropomorphism means attributing human characteristics to something non-human. Perceived empathy concerns whether a chatbot’s responses seem understanding and emotionally appropriate to the user.

Emotional attachment describes an AI becoming personally meaningful through comfort, familiarity, or connection. Romantic attraction concerns romantic interest toward an AI system or persona. For this project, attachment does not automatically mean romance, and perceived empathy does not establish that a system actually feels empathy.

Keeping separate measures matters because I want the later study to allow different combinations. Someone might report comfort without romantic attraction. Someone else might enjoy a romantic persona while fully recognising that it is artificial.

What the five hypotheses propose

The first hypothesis predicts an association between loneliness and emotional attachment. The second asks whether anthropomorphism is associated with attachment after accounting for loneliness and frequency of AI use. The third predicts a relationship between perceived empathy and attachment.

The fourth asks whether stronger attachment tends to occur alongside stronger romantic attraction. The fifth is exploratory: would the relationship between loneliness and romantic attraction become weaker after accounting for attachment, anthropomorphism, and perceived empathy?

These are predictions. Writing them clearly makes it possible to examine whether the responses support them, rather than changing the explanation to fit whatever appears later.

The model has to allow other explanations

I also discuss ways the proposed framework could be incomplete. Perhaps someone who already feels attached rates the chatbot as more empathetic. Perhaps usage frequency explains part of an apparent relationship. Perhaps a particular system’s design matters more than a user characteristic.

Those possibilities affect what a result would mean. If empathy and attachment are related in a one-time survey, I cannot use that alone to say which came first. A diagram can organise a proposed pathway, but its arrows do not establish causation.

What I’m learning from the first stage

This has pushed me to slow down and explain what I actually mean. An interesting explanation needs clear definitions, measurable questions, and room to be challenged. Paper I is where I’m doing that groundwork before the planned empirical stage.

The goal is a framework I can test and improve. Whether every hypothesis survives is a question for the next part of the project.
