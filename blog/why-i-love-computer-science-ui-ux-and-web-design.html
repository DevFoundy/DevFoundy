Why I Love Computer Science, UI/UX, and Web Design

From experimenting with AI to building my own projects — my journey into software development and why I believe technology should be accessible to everyone.

By Kenta | Founder of DevFoundry·November 5, 2026·7 min read

Where It All Started

My interest in technology didn't begin when I was a little kid writing complicated programs. In fact, it started fairly recently, in 2025.

That was around the time AI-assisted coding, often called vibe coding, was becoming increasingly popular. Tools like Claude were making it possible for people to turn ideas into working software without having to write every single line of code themselves.

What fascinated me wasn't just how powerful AI was becoming. It was the idea that something which might traditionally take a developer hours or even days to create could now be developed much faster with the help of AI.

Suddenly, building software felt much more accessible.

But my interest in technology actually came from somewhere quite different: theatre.

In early 2025, I joined my school's theatre production team. I got to observe the senior technical team members working with different systems and equipment, and they started teaching me how everything worked.

Seeing how technology could bring an entire production together made me curious about what else was possible.

That curiosity eventually led me toward programming.

My First Project Was Far From Perfect

My first real software project was a fitness-tracking website called KineticForm.

And to be completely transparent, it wasn't very good.

At the time, I barely understood how programming worked. I didn't have much experience with software development, and I was still figuring out the fundamentals of technology itself.

But I was building something.

That mattered more to me than whether the project looked professional or whether the code was perfect.

As I started learning about Python and reading more about programming, I began to realize that software development was something I genuinely wanted to pursue.

I wasn't necessarily fascinated by the act of typing code. I was fascinated by what code could make possible.

To this day, that's still what interests me most about computer science: creating things and understanding how systems work.

Turning the Impossible Into Something Real

One of my favorite things about software development is the freedom it gives me.

When I'm building my own projects, I can experiment with ideas without someone else deciding what I'm allowed to create.

I can imagine a feature, research how it might work, experiment with different approaches, and eventually turn that idea into something functional.

Of course, there are technical limitations. Sometimes I run into problems I don't understand, particularly when working with more advanced concepts such as APIs and AI integration.

But those challenges are part of the learning process.

One of my biggest projects, StageWrite Studio, taught me an important lesson:

Something that seems impossible at first might simply be something I haven't learned how to build yet.

That's probably the most satisfying part of development for me.

It's the moment when an idea that once seemed unrealistic actually starts working.

Why UI/UX and Web Design Matter to Me

My interest in UI/UX design started when I came across a design competition on social media.

It introduced me to another side of software development: not just how applications work, but how people experience them.

Personally, I enjoy designing interfaces even more than writing code.

That doesn't mean I think functionality is unimportant. Quite the opposite.

I believe a website can look incredible and still be a bad website.

You could have beautiful animations, modern typography, and impressive visual effects. But if the website is slow, difficult to navigate, or full of buttons that don't work, what's the point?

A professional website isn't just one that looks good. It's one that works well for the people using it.

That's the principle I try to follow when designing my own projects.

I often prefer minimalist, clean interfaces, sometimes similar to Apple's design approach. But I don't think every project should look the same.

Different products have different purposes, audiences, and personalities. Good design should reflect that.

Ultimately, I want people using something I've created to feel comfortable, in control, and free to make it their own.

My Perspective on AI-Assisted Coding

AI-assisted coding is something I strongly support, and I think it's important to be transparent about that.

I don't believe being a software developer should be defined exclusively by how much code someone can write from memory.

To me, software development also involves identifying problems, designing solutions, understanding systems, communicating ideas clearly, and making decisions about how everything should work together.

Writing code is an important part of that process, but it isn't the entire process.

AI tools have made it easier for people like me to experiment with software development and bring ideas to life.

That doesn't mean AI automatically produces perfect software. You still need to understand what you're building, evaluate the results, identify problems, and keep learning.

I certainly still have a lot to learn, especially when it comes to more advanced programming concepts.

But I see AI as a tool that expands what developers can achieve, rather than something that makes learning unnecessary.

And as someone interested in pursuing AI engineering and software development in the future, I'm excited to see where these technologies go.

Why I Started DevFoundry

DevFoundry wasn't an idea that appeared overnight.

For a long time, I wanted to create something of my own that could help other people learn, build, and experiment with technology.

Two projects I'm particularly proud of are OpenBridge and DevFoundry.

They're meaningful to me because they represent ideas I had wanted to pursue for a while, even before I felt ready to make them happen.

One of the biggest reasons I created DevFoundry was something I kept noticing while looking for programming resources online.

There are countless courses available, but many of the more comprehensive ones require payment.

Free resources certainly exist, and some are excellent. However, I also came across plenty that felt incomplete, overly basic, or didn't provide enough practical experience.

I wanted to contribute something different.

I believe people should have access to useful, in-depth programming education regardless of whether they can afford expensive courses.

That belief became an important part of what I wanted DevFoundry to represent.

Not just building websites, but also sharing knowledge and helping others develop their own skills.

Why Open Source Matters

Another thing I strongly believe in is open-source software.

When I create something, I don't necessarily want people to use it exactly as I designed it.

I want them to be able to explore it, understand how it works, and modify it to suit their own needs.

Maybe someone likes the functionality of a website but wants a completely different design.

Maybe they want to add features I never considered.

Or maybe they're just learning to code and want to see how a real project is structured.

That's what I find exciting about making code available to others.

Software shouldn't always be something people can only use. It can also be something they learn from and build upon.

And when people are free to experiment, they might create something even better.

My Advice to Anyone Starting Out

If you're around my age and interested in programming, my advice is simple:

Start small, learn the basics, and experiment with what you already know.

You don't need to begin with an enormous project or understand every programming language.

I'd recommend starting with beginner Python tutorials on YouTube.

Once you've learned a few concepts, try making something yourself.

It doesn't have to be impressive.

My first project certainly wasn't.

The important thing is to take what you've learned and actually use it.

You will run into problems. You will encounter concepts you don't understand. And you'll probably build things that don't work properly the first time.

That's normal.

I still experience those challenges myself.

But every project gives you another opportunity to understand something new.

This Is Only the Beginning

I'm still early in my journey as a developer.

There are plenty of technologies I haven't explored, programming concepts I don't fully understand, and projects I haven't figured out how to build yet.

In the future, I'd love to work more deeply in AI engineering and software development.

But regardless of where that takes me, I want to keep creating things, experimenting with ideas, and sharing what I learn along the way.

Because ultimately, my interest in computer science isn't just about programming languages, frameworks, or tools.

It's about what technology allows people to create.

And that's also why I started DevFoundry.

I build software because I believe learning software development and web design should be accessible to everyone, and that knowledge is worth sharing freely.

Thanks for reading my story.

— Kenta, Founder of DevFoundry
