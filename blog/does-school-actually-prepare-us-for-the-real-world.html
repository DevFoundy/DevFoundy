Does School Actually Prepare Us for the Real World?

A student’s view of exams, subject choice, and why schools should make more room for practical projects and independent research.

By Kenta | Founder of DevFoundry·November 11, 2026·20 min read

We spend years of our lives in classrooms, studying different subjects, completing assignments, and preparing for examinations. But how much of what we learn actually prepares us for the world outside school?

This is something I've been thinking about quite a lot recently.

As a student, I spend a significant amount of my time studying subjects ranging from Mathematics and Science to Computer Science and Psychology. Some of these subjects genuinely interest me, while others make me question why I'm required to study them in the first place.

Don't get me wrong. I don't think school is useless, and I certainly don't believe that education is unimportant. In fact, I think education is one of the most valuable things a person can receive.

However, I do question whether the way we currently approach education is necessarily the best way to prepare students for their futures.

We live in a world where technology is constantly evolving, careers are changing, and new opportunities are appearing. Yet much of our education still revolves around following a curriculum, memorising information, and achieving the highest possible examination grades.

And that makes me wonder:

Are we teaching students how to succeed in life, or are we mainly teaching them how to succeed in school?

Not Every Subject Is Equally Useful to Every Student

One thing I genuinely appreciate about school is that some subjects teach us skills we can apply directly to everyday life.

For example, subjects such as Personal, Social, Health and Economic Education (PSHE) and personal finance can help students understand important aspects of adulthood.

Learning how to manage money, make responsible decisions, maintain our health, and navigate everyday challenges is something almost everyone can benefit from, regardless of their future career.

These are the kinds of subjects I believe should remain compulsory.

However, I feel differently about certain other subjects.

Imagine a student who has already decided that they want to become a scientist. They're passionate about Biology, Chemistry, and Physics, and they genuinely enjoy exploring scientific concepts.

Yet their school might still require them to spend time studying subjects such as robotics, even if those subjects aren't particularly relevant to their interests.

Now, I'm not suggesting that robotics or technology is unimportant. Quite the opposite. Technology plays an enormous role in modern society, including scientific research.

But shouldn't students have more freedom to decide what they want to specialise in?

If someone is genuinely interested in becoming a scientist, wouldn't it make sense to give them more opportunities to explore science rather than requiring them to dedicate equal amounts of time to subjects they have little interest in?

Of course, younger students may not know what they want to do in the future, which is why I understand the importance of introducing everyone to a broad range of subjects.

But as students grow older and begin developing their own interests, I believe schools should gradually give them more control over their education.

Education should help students discover and develop their strengths, not force everyone to follow exactly the same path.

Imagine Asking Every Animal to Climb a Tree

There's a popular analogy about education that I think illustrates this problem particularly well.

Imagine placing several different animals in front of a tree.

There's a monkey, a fish, an elephant, and a bird.

Now imagine telling all of them that they must complete the same examination: climb the tree.

Naturally, the monkey would probably perform extremely well. Climbing is something it's physically adapted to do.

The fish, on the other hand, wouldn't stand much of a chance.

But does that mean the fish is less capable than the monkey?

Of course not.

The fish simply has different abilities.

If the examination involved swimming instead, the results would look completely different.

I think something similar happens in education.

Every student has different strengths, weaknesses, interests, and ways of learning. Some students are excellent at memorising information. Others are better at practical problem-solving, communication, creativity, or independent research.

Yet we often evaluate them through very similar methods.

Written examinations, standardised questions, and mark schemes are designed to measure whether students have achieved particular learning objectives.

And while I understand why these systems exist, I don't think they always capture the full range of what a student is capable of.

A student who struggles with written examinations might be excellent at designing something, leading a team, or solving problems in practical situations.

Another student might perform exceptionally well in examinations but have less experience applying that knowledge outside a classroom.

Neither student is necessarily better than the other.

They're simply demonstrating different abilities.

The problem begins when we treat one particular method of assessment as though it represents someone's entire intelligence or potential.

The Problem With Examination Mark Schemes

This is something I've personally experienced, particularly in Computer Science.

Computer Science is one of the subjects I'm genuinely interested in. I enjoy understanding how software works, exploring programming concepts, and developing applications.

However, I've noticed something frustrating when it comes to examinations.

Sometimes, I feel confident that I understand the content being tested. I know what the question is asking, and I can explain the concept in my own words.

Yet when I receive my results, I discover that I've lost marks because my explanation didn't match what the mark scheme expected.

Now, I understand that examination boards need mark schemes.

Without them, it would be extremely difficult to assess thousands of students consistently and fairly.

I also understand that Computer Science requires technical accuracy. Certain terms have specific meanings, and using the wrong terminology can sometimes indicate a misunderstanding.

But I think there's an important distinction between genuinely misunderstanding a concept and understanding it without expressing it in the particular way an examiner expects.

For example, imagine two students answering a question about how a computer system works.

One student has memorised the textbook definition and reproduces it almost perfectly.

The other student understands the concept through practical experience and explains it using different terminology.

If both explanations demonstrate the same accurate understanding, shouldn't both receive appropriate credit?

In principle, mark schemes are supposed to recognise equivalent correct answers, not just identical wording. But in practice, I still feel that the way we prepare for examinations can place too much emphasis on learning how to phrase answers for marks.

Sometimes, studying starts to feel less like learning a subject and more like learning how to satisfy an examiner.

And that's what concerns me.

Because outside school, problems don't usually come with a mark scheme.

When you're developing software, conducting research, or trying to solve a real problem, there isn't necessarily one predefined answer that everyone must reproduce.

You have to experiment, analyse the situation, make decisions, and sometimes discover solutions that nobody has shown you before.

I believe education should place more value on demonstrating understanding and applying knowledge, rather than focusing so heavily on how students express that knowledge in examinations.

Are High Grades Really a Measure of Future Success?

There's an idea I've come across suggesting that students who achieve the highest grades eventually become employees, while students who perform less successfully in school become entrepreneurs or CEOs.

I think the message behind this idea is interesting, although I wouldn't take it literally.

Students who consistently achieve A or A* grades often demonstrate qualities such as discipline, dedication, organisation, and the ability to follow instructions.

Those are genuinely valuable skills.

In fact, achieving excellent academic results requires a considerable amount of effort, and I respect students who are able to maintain that level of performance.

However, I also think there are students who receive lower grades for reasons that aren't necessarily related to their intelligence.

Some students might struggle to concentrate during traditional lessons.

Others might prefer learning independently, experimenting with ideas, or working on practical projects rather than completing written assignments.

And some might simply feel restricted by an environment where they're constantly expected to follow specific instructions.

That doesn't automatically mean they're future entrepreneurs or that they're more creative than academically successful students.

Likewise, achieving excellent grades doesn't mean someone lacks creativity or independence.

There are plenty of people who are both academically successful and highly innovative.

My point is that academic performance and real-world capability aren't exactly the same thing.

Grades can tell us something about a student's understanding, preparation, and performance under particular assessment conditions.

But they don't necessarily tell us everything about that student's creativity, leadership, communication skills, initiative, or ability to solve unfamiliar problems.

Imagine two students applying for a software development position.

One has outstanding examination results and a strong understanding of programming theory.

The other has average academic results but has spent years building applications, collaborating with others, and solving practical development problems.

Which student would be more suitable?

I don't think there's a universal answer.

It depends on the position, the skills required, and what each student can demonstrate.

Ideally, someone would have both strong theoretical knowledge and practical experience.

But that raises another question.

If practical experience is so valuable, why don't schools provide more opportunities for students to develop it?

What If Students Had More Freedom to Choose?

If I had the opportunity to redesign a secondary school curriculum, one of the first things I would change would be how much freedom students have over their subjects.

Personally, I would allow students to choose almost everything they study, particularly as they get older.

However, I wouldn't make every subject optional.

I believe there are certain areas of knowledge that everyone should understand, regardless of their career ambitions.

Personal finance is one example.

Students should understand how money works, how to budget, how to manage their finances responsibly, and how to make informed financial decisions.

Health education is another.

Understanding physical and mental well-being, personal safety, and healthy decision-making is relevant to everyone.

I would also retain language education because communication is such an important part of everyday life.

But one subject I would particularly like to introduce is Critical Thinking.

Instead of focusing compulsory education almost entirely on traditional academic subjects, I think students should spend more time learning how to think logically, evaluate evidence, identify misleading information, and approach unfamiliar problems.

For example, rather than only learning how to solve increasingly complicated mathematical equations, students could also explore how mathematical reasoning applies to everyday situations.

How do you evaluate whether a claim is reliable?

How do you make a decision when you don't have complete information?

How do you recognise flaws in an argument?

How do you approach a problem that doesn't have an obvious solution?

These are skills that can be useful in almost any profession.

Of course, I'm not suggesting that Mathematics should disappear.

Mathematics is essential for many careers, including engineering, science, economics, and technology. Even everyday life requires a foundation in numeracy.

But I think there's room to reconsider how much advanced Mathematics should be compulsory for students who don't intend to pursue mathematically intensive fields.

Students who want to specialise in Mathematics should absolutely have the opportunity to study it in greater depth.

Meanwhile, other students could focus on practical numeracy, logical reasoning, and subjects that align more closely with their interests.

The goal wouldn't be to make education easier.

It would be to make education more relevant to the individual student.

Teachers Aren't Necessarily the Problem

Whenever people criticise education, I feel like teachers often receive a significant amount of the blame.

Students complain that lessons are boring, assignments are unnecessary, or examinations are too difficult.

But I don't think it's fair to assume that teachers are always responsible for these problems.

In many cases, teachers are simply working within the requirements of the curriculum they're expected to deliver.

They have specific topics to cover, learning objectives to meet, and examinations to prepare students for.

Even if a teacher believes that a more practical or creative approach would help students learn, they may not always have enough time or flexibility to use it.

Imagine a Science teacher who wants to spend several lessons allowing students to design their own experiments.

That could be an excellent opportunity for students to develop research skills, learn through trial and error, and become more curious about science.

But if the teacher is under pressure to complete the syllabus before an upcoming examination, those lessons might not be practical to organise.

Instead, the teacher may need to focus on delivering the required content as efficiently as possible.

And I think that's unfortunate.

Because I believe many teachers genuinely want their students to enjoy learning.

They want students to ask questions, explore ideas, and develop an interest in their subjects.

But the structure of the education system doesn't always give them the freedom to prioritise those experiences.

This is why I think meaningful change needs to go beyond individual classrooms.

We need to reconsider what we're asking teachers to achieve in the first place.

The World Has Changed. Has Education Changed Enough?

Think about how much the world has evolved over the past few decades.

Cars have become more advanced.

Computers have transformed from relatively specialised machines into devices that almost everyone uses.

The internet has completely changed how we communicate, access information, and work.

Artificial intelligence is now capable of assisting with tasks that would have seemed incredibly difficult to automate not long ago.

Entire industries have emerged, while existing professions continue to evolve.

Yet if you compare many classrooms today with classrooms from several decades ago, you'll still recognise much of the same structure.

Students sit at desks.

Teachers deliver lessons.

Assignments are completed.

Examinations are taken.

Grades are awarded.

Of course, education hasn't remained completely unchanged.

We now have digital learning platforms, interactive resources, online research tools, and many different teaching approaches that weren't available to previous generations.

But I sometimes wonder whether we've focused too much on changing the tools used in education rather than reconsidering its underlying structure.

For example, replacing a paper worksheet with a digital worksheet might make the process more convenient.

But if students are still completing exactly the same type of task, has the learning experience fundamentally changed?

Similarly, giving every student a laptop doesn't automatically make a lesson more innovative.

Technology can improve education, but only when we use it to create better learning opportunities.

I think the bigger question is whether our curricula and assessment methods are keeping pace with the skills students will need in the future.

If the world is constantly changing, shouldn't education be designed to adapt alongside it?

Why I Think Schools Need More Hands-On Learning

If there's one thing I would prioritise in education, it would be practical experience.

I genuinely believe schools should provide more opportunities for students to work on independent projects, conduct research, and apply what they've learned.

Imagine a Computer Science class where students spend part of the year developing their own applications.

They could identify a problem, design a solution, write code, test their software, and present the finished product.

Along the way, they would learn about programming, debugging, software design, problem-solving, and project management.

And perhaps more importantly, they would experience what it's actually like to develop something.

Now imagine a Science class where students are encouraged to investigate their own research questions.

Rather than only following predefined experiments, they could develop hypotheses, design investigations, collect data, and evaluate their findings.

They might discover that their original predictions were incorrect.

But that's part of learning.

In real scientific research, experiments don't always produce the results we expect.

And I think understanding how to respond to unexpected outcomes is just as important as knowing the correct answer to a textbook question.

The same idea could apply to many other subjects.

Business students could develop and manage small projects.

Literature students could explore creative writing and independent analysis.

Students interested in design could create products or solve practical design challenges.

These experiences wouldn't need to replace traditional lessons entirely.

Instead, they could become a more significant part of the curriculum.

Students would still learn the necessary theory, but they would also have opportunities to apply it.

Personally, I find that working on projects can teach me things that I might not fully understand from reading a textbook alone.

When something doesn't work, I have to investigate why.

When I encounter a problem, I have to search for possible solutions.

And when I finally get something working, I gain a better understanding of how the different parts fit together.

That process is valuable because it encourages independence.

Instead of always waiting for someone to explain the next step, students begin learning how to figure things out themselves.

And I think that's one of the most important abilities education can develop.

Research Shouldn't Be Reserved for Universities

Another change I'd like to see is giving younger students more opportunities to conduct independent research.

When people hear the phrase research paper, they often associate it with universities, professional scientists, or academics.

But I don't think research needs to begin at university.

Students can learn valuable research skills long before they reach higher education.

For example, a student interested in Psychology could investigate a question about human behaviour.

Someone interested in technology could explore how different algorithms perform under particular conditions.

A student passionate about environmental science could study a local environmental issue and collect relevant data.

The research wouldn't necessarily need to produce a groundbreaking discovery.

That isn't the point.

The value comes from learning how to ask meaningful questions, evaluate sources, develop methods, collect evidence, and draw reasonable conclusions.

Students would also learn that not every idea turns out to be correct.

Sometimes, a hypothesis is unsupported.

Sometimes, an experiment doesn't work.

And sometimes, the available evidence simply isn't enough to reach a confident conclusion.

Those aren't necessarily failures.

They're opportunities to understand how knowledge is developed.

I think schools could benefit from treating independent research as an important part of education rather than something only a small number of students pursue outside their regular classes.

Education Shouldn't Be About Making Everyone the Same

One concern I have with highly standardised education is that students can begin to feel as though there's only one correct way to succeed.

Study the syllabus.

Complete the assignments.

Memorise the required information.

Achieve high grades.

Repeat.

For some students, this system works extremely well.

But for others, it might not reflect how they learn best or what they're capable of achieving.

I don't think the solution is to remove standards or stop assessing students.

Schools still need ways to ensure that students are learning and developing essential knowledge.

However, I believe we should allow more flexibility in how students demonstrate that learning.

For example, why couldn't certain subjects assess students through a combination of examinations, practical projects, presentations, and independent investigations?

A student who struggles to communicate their understanding through a written examination might demonstrate it effectively through a presentation or practical task.

Another student might prefer a traditional examination.

Neither method is automatically superior.

They simply assess different skills.

Of course, designing fair assessments across different formats would be challenging.

Schools would need clear criteria, consistent expectations, and ways to ensure that students genuinely completed their own work.

But I don't think those challenges mean the idea isn't worth exploring.

After all, education is supposed to help students develop.

It shouldn't simply be about sorting them into categories based on how successfully they perform in one particular environment.

So, Does School Actually Prepare Us for the Real World?

I think the answer is both yes and no.

School absolutely provides valuable knowledge.

It teaches us foundational academic concepts, introduces us to different subjects, and gives us opportunities to interact with people from different backgrounds.

It can also help students develop discipline, responsibility, communication skills, and the ability to work towards long-term goals.

Those things matter.

But I don't think academic knowledge alone is enough.

The world outside school is complicated.

Problems don't always have predefined solutions.

People don't always agree with each other.

Projects don't always go according to plan.

And sometimes, the most important lessons come from trying something, making mistakes, and figuring out what to do next.

I believe schools should prepare students for those situations as well.

If I could change one thing about the education system, I would redesign the curriculum to place greater emphasis on hands-on learning.

I would give students more freedom to choose their subjects, more opportunities to pursue independent projects, and more chances to explore the things they're genuinely curious about.

I would also encourage schools to assess students through a wider range of methods rather than relying so heavily on written examinations.

This doesn't mean abandoning academic standards.

It means recognising that education can serve a broader purpose than preparing students to achieve excellent grades.

Ultimately, I don't think the goal of school should be to produce students who are simply good at being students.

It should be to help people become curious, capable, independent individuals who know how to learn, adapt, and solve problems long after they've left the classroom.

Because at the end of the day, an examination might test what you know at a particular moment.

But life will constantly challenge you to figure out what you don't know yet.

And personally, I think that's what education should prepare us for.
