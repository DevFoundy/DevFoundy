7 Signs Your Website Needs an Upgrade

A useful website should make the next step easier. Here’s how to spot what’s getting in the way.

By Kenta·November 4, 2026·6 min read

A website can still be online and quietly make life difficult for the people using it. A menu opens awkwardly on a phone. A contact form looks fine but never delivers the message. The business has changed, while the homepage tells an older story.

An upgrade doesn’t always mean starting again. Often, a few focused changes can make the existing site much more useful. Start by checking these seven signs, then fix the problems that stop visitors completing their main task.

1. It’s awkward to use on a phone

If visitors have to pinch to read, scroll sideways, or tap several times to hit a tiny link, the mobile layout needs attention. A desktop page squeezed onto a small screen can hide the information people came for.

Quick check: Open the site on your phone and try a real task: read a service description, find a price, or get in touch. Open the menu and use the form, including the on-screen keyboard.

Start here: Let columns stack, keep text readable without zooming, and give controls enough space to tap comfortably. Check that images fit their containers and that sticky banners don’t cover buttons or form fields.

2. Visitors wait for the useful part to load

A page may appear quickly but still feel slow if the main image arrives late, text shifts around, or buttons take time to respond. Testing only on your own fast connection can hide that experience.

Quick check: Load a few important pages on a mobile connection. Notice when you can actually read and interact, then use a performance report to investigate the delay. Repeat the test rather than judging everything from one score.

Start here: Resize and compress oversized images, remove scripts you no longer use, and review heavy embeds. Load images further down the page when needed, while keeping the main visible content a priority. Measure again after each change. The web.dev performance guides explain how to investigate specific bottlenecks.

3. The design tells an outdated story

A site doesn’t need to follow every design trend. It does need to represent what you offer today. Old screenshots, inconsistent typography, crowded sections, and services you no longer provide can make visitors question whether the information is current.

Quick check: Look at the homepage as a first-time visitor. Can you explain who the site is for and what it offers after reading the opening section? Compare the examples and descriptions with your current work.

Start here: Update the content before choosing new colours. Replace stale examples, simplify competing styles, and use a consistent type scale and spacing. A clearer hierarchy can make an existing design feel much more considered.

4. People struggle to find what they need

If basic information is buried behind vague menu labels or several unnecessary clicks, the navigation needs work. You may know that “Discover” contains your services; a new visitor has to guess.

Quick check: Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to find a service, an example of your work, and a way to contact you. Watch where they hesitate without explaining the route.

Start here: Use familiar labels such as “Services”, “Work”, and “Contact”. Group related pages, remove duplicate destinations, and provide a clear route back. Make sure the mobile menu offers the same essential paths.

5. Links, forms, or features don’t finish the job

A button that leads nowhere is easy to spot. A form that announces success without delivering anything is harder. Broken downloads, missing images, and outdated booking links can interrupt an otherwise convincing site.

Quick check: Follow the main visitor journeys from start to finish. On forms you own, send a clearly labelled test submission and confirm it reaches the intended destination. Try an invalid entry too: does the error explain how to recover?

Start here: Fix broken destinations, explain unavailable features, and give clear feedback after an action. Test again after changing a form provider, page address, or integration. Keep a short checklist of the journeys that matter most.

6. Some visitors can’t comfortably read or operate it

Faint text, missing image descriptions, unlabeled fields, and controls that only work with a mouse create barriers. Accessibility needs attention throughout the page, including menus, forms, and messages.

Quick check: Move through the site with Tab and Shift+Tab, activate controls with the keyboard, and check that focus stays visible. Enlarge the text and look for content that becomes clipped. Review headings, meaningful image alternatives, form labels, and contrast.

Start here: Fix the barriers you find and use semantic headings and standard controls. W3C’s Easy Checks for accessibility offers a useful starting point. These checks and automated tools can reveal issues; passing them alone doesn’t establish that the whole site is accessible.

7. The next step is unclear

A visitor may understand your service and still leave unsure what to do. Several equally prominent buttons, vague “Click here” links, or a contact option hidden far below the relevant content can make that decision harder.

Quick check: Choose a key page and name its main action. Does the page clearly invite that action, and does the button’s destination match its promise?

Start here: Choose a primary next step and label it specifically: “Request a website quote”, “View the project”, or “Book an introductory call”. Explain what happens next and ask only for the information needed to begin.

Choose the first fix

Write down each issue, the page it affects, and what it prevents someone from doing. Repair blocked tasks first, then tackle recurring friction, then refine the visual details. Save a copy before making changes and repeat the same visitor task afterwards to see whether the fix helped.

If the structure and content still serve the business, focused improvements may be enough. A wider rebuild becomes worth considering when the site’s foundations repeatedly prevent the changes you need.
