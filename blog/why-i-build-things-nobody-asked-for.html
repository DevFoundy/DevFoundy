Why I Build Things Nobody Asked For

Why I build software for myself, share it as open source, and value the experience of creating more than popularity or profit.

Intouch Kenta TakizawaNovember 20, 20265 min read

Whenever people talk about software development, the conversation often revolves around innovation, profitability, market demand and attracting users.

But what if I simply want to build something for myself?

At this point in my journey as a software developer, most of my projects aren't designed to become successful businesses. They aren't created because thousands of people are asking for them, nor because I've discovered an untapped market.

Most of the time, I build things because I want them to exist.

Solving My Own Problems

For me, software development is currently about creating things that I personally want or that solve problems I experience.

There have been numerous occasions where I've discovered an application that offers exactly what I'm looking for, only to realise that many of its useful features are locked behind a subscription.

Instead of spending money on another application, I often find myself asking a simple question: why not build my own?

Of course, developing an application takes considerably more time than downloading an existing one. It can involve hours of debugging, redesigning interfaces and fixing problems that I never anticipated.

But that's also part of what makes it enjoyable.

Not only do I eventually get an application tailored to my preferences, but I also gain experience throughout the process.

How Sleeve Came to Exist

One of my latest projects, Sleeve, is a good example of this philosophy.

As someone who enjoys listening to music, I wanted a platform where I could discover, rate and keep track of songs.

While there were already applications that offered similar functionality, some required payment to access certain features.

Rather than paying for another service, I decided to develop my own.

Sleeve wasn't created because I believed the world desperately needed another music-rating platform. It was created because I wanted one that worked the way I imagined it.

The fact that other people might find it useful is an additional benefit.

I don't necessarily need to outperform established applications. I just need to create something that fulfils the purpose I originally intended.

Why I Believe in Open Source

Personally, I believe that open-source development is something software developers should embrace more often.

One of the reasons I make my projects publicly available is that not everyone has access to the same development resources.

AI-assisted development has opened countless opportunities for people to create software. However, many of the more capable development tools still require paid subscriptions, and not everyone can afford them.

By making my projects open source, I can give other people access to something they might not have had the resources to develop themselves.

More importantly, they don't have to use my applications exactly as I designed them.

They can modify the interface, introduce new features, remove unnecessary functionality or completely transform the original project into something that better suits their needs.

I might create an application to solve my own problem, but someone else could take that same application and use it to solve an entirely different one.

That's one of the things I find most interesting about open-source software.

Not Everything Needs to Make Money

I understand why developers charge for their software.

Development takes time, effort and, in some cases, significant financial investment. Servers, APIs, hosting and development tools aren't always free.

I'm not against monetisation, and there may be circumstances in the future where I need to recover money that I've personally invested into a project.

However, whenever possible, I would prefer to keep my applications free and provide an optional donation system.

If someone finds one of my projects useful and wants to support its development, I would genuinely appreciate that. But I wouldn't want people to feel obligated to pay me simply to benefit from something I've created.

For now, financial success isn't the main reason I develop software.

Experience is.

What If Nobody Uses My Application?

One of the questions that developers might ask themselves is whether anyone will actually use their project.

Personally, I don't worry about that too much.

If I spend dozens of hours developing an application and nobody ends up using it other than me, I wouldn't necessarily consider it a failure.

I've still gained experience. I've still learned something new. Most importantly, I've created something that solves one of my own problems.

We often associate the success of software with download counts, active users, revenue and popularity.

While those measurements are important in commercial development, I don't think they should define every personal project.

An application with a single user can still be useful.

Even if that single user happens to be its developer.

Building for Myself, Sharing With Everyone

One of the advantages of developing software independently is that I don't have to constantly consider what everyone else wants.

I can create features that might seem unnecessary to other people, experiment with different interfaces or completely redesign an application because I feel like trying something new.

That freedom is something I genuinely appreciate.

Of course, my preferences won't always align with everyone else's. A feature that I find incredibly useful might be something another person never touches.

That's precisely why I believe in making my projects open source.

Rather than attempting to design an application that satisfies absolutely everyone, I can create a foundation that others are free to adapt.

I don't have to make every decision for every possible user.

The Future of DevFoundry

Although DevFoundry currently serves as a platform for my personal projects, articles and experiments, I hope it eventually becomes something much larger.

I would like DevFoundry to become an ecosystem where people can access free software, educational resources and open-source projects.

This is also part of the motivation behind DevFoundry Academy.

There are currently two courses planned for release, and I hope to expand the available educational content over time.

I don't know exactly how large DevFoundry will become or what direction it will ultimately take. However, I would like it to develop into an organisation that encourages people to create, experiment, learn and share their knowledge with others.

For now, I'm simply building things one project at a time.

Build Anything You Want

If there's one piece of advice I would give to someone interested in software development, it would be to build whatever you want.

It doesn't matter if your idea already exists.

It doesn't matter if another developer has created something better.

It doesn't matter if your application never becomes popular or generates revenue.

You don't even have to finish it.

An unfinished project can still teach you something. A failed experiment can still introduce you to a new concept. An application that nobody downloads can still solve a meaningful problem.

Not everything you create needs to become your greatest achievement.

Sometimes, the experience of creating something is valuable enough on its own.

And sometimes, the best reason to build something is simply because you want it to exist.
