A Mind Full of Ideas, but Never Enough Time

On capturing personal concepts, building beyond my current skills, refining ideas with AI, and why having more ideas than time is still a blessing.

DevFoundry Founder’s JournalNovember 13, 20267 min read

I have so many concepts that even choosing one to talk about can be difficult. There are things I want to build, systems I want to explore, and ideas I want to develop further. Each one has something that makes me want to spend time on it. The difficulty is finding enough time to give those ideas the attention they deserve.

That can be disappointing. I can imagine how something might work and understand why I would want it, while also knowing that bringing it to life would take more time or knowledge than I currently have. The excitement is real, but so is the gap between having a concept and being able to build it.

That gap is something I keep coming back to. My imagination does not wait for an opening in my schedule. Ideas arrive when they arrive, whether or not I am ready to do anything with them.

Capturing an idea while it is fresh

When a new concept comes to mind, I try to write down the details immediately, while they are still fresh. I want to capture more than a title. The details are often what make the idea interesting: what it should do, how its parts might connect, and why I would want to use it.

If it is clearly a project that would require a lot of time, I store the information somewhere I can return to later. Writing it down lets me preserve the concept without having to begin development straight away. I might not be ready to build it, but I can at least keep enough of the thought to understand it again.

There is some comfort in that, although saving an idea does not remove the disappointment of postponing it. I still want to see what it could become. Having the concept recorded is useful; having something I can actually use would feel different. Sometimes, I simply feel bummed that I cannot work on it.

Why the ideas feel personal

Much of that feeling comes from how personal my ideas are. I mainly develop projects for myself. They are tailored to things I need, things I would find useful, or ways I would prefer something to work. I have a reason to care about the details because those details affect whether the result would help me.

That also shapes how I think about originality. I am less concerned about someone independently arriving at the exact same concept because my version grows out of my own needs. Someone else might explore similar territory, but the particular combination I have in mind feels personal. The more immediate concern is whether I will have the time and ability to make it real.

Leaving room for other people

Building primarily for myself does not mean I want the result to be useful only to me. I also want other people to be able to use my open-source projects and tune them to their liking. My preferences can provide a starting point while leaving room for someone else to adapt the software to their own needs.

I like that possibility. I do not have to anticipate every preference or decide exactly how everyone should use a project. Someone else may want a different arrangement, different behaviour, or a capability I have not prioritised. Giving them room to make those changes allows the project to serve purposes beyond the one that originally motivated me.

When the concept is ahead of my skills

Of course, even a very personal and clearly defined concept can be difficult to implement. A lot of my projects are above my current skill level or require more time than I can realistically give them. Understanding what I want does not automatically tell me how to build it.

Games are a good example. A concept can involve many variables, including random ones, and a lot of behaviour that needs to work together. I can imagine the experience I want, but implementing all the interactions is another challenge. As the number of moving parts grows, so does the amount I need to understand.

Projects involving embedded systems, APIs, and integrations can be difficult for similar reasons. There are more connections to work through, and each part brings its own requirements. What sounds like one feature in a description may depend on several components working together properly. The concept can be clear while the path to making it function remains uncertain.

That is where the limits of time and knowledge overlap. I need time to learn, time to implement what I have learned, and time to work through whatever I did not anticipate. It is frustrating when an idea is exciting enough to keep thinking about but demanding enough that I cannot reasonably pursue it yet.

How AI helps me refine the idea

AI has helped me improve my ideas considerably. It gives me a way to develop a rough concept further, explore possibilities, and refine what I am trying to achieve. Something that begins as an incomplete thought can become much clearer through that process.

I still care about the human motivation behind the work. My projects come from wanting something to exist for a reason that matters to me. AI can contribute unexpected suggestions and novel combinations, and I do not think I need to dismiss those contributions to value my own originality. What matters is that I remain involved in deciding what is worth building and why.

For me, that involvement is essential. I want to recognise my intentions in the result. An idea becoming more polished or technically ambitious is useful when those improvements serve the reason I wanted to create it. I still need to judge whether a suggestion belongs in the project, whether it helps, and whether it makes the experience better.

Keeping depth without overwhelming people

Sometimes, a project becomes overly complicated. There can be too many options in a single box, too many settings competing for attention, or too much for someone to work through at once. At that point, the difficulty extends beyond implementation. It affects the experience of using what I have built.

My preference is to keep advanced options available for the people who need them. I do not want to remove useful control simply because presenting everything together has become overwhelming. I would rather organise it so that the essential choices are clear and the more detailed settings remain accessible.

Progressive disclosure describes that approach well: let people begin with what they need, then reveal more depth when they want it. A person should be able to use the main features without having to understand every possible adjustment first. Someone who wants greater control should still be able to find it. That gives me a way to retain the ambition of a project while making it easier to approach.

Returning with more to give

There are also times when I reach a point where I cannot see how to improve a project further, or I do not know how to make the improvement I want. That can feel like reaching the top of what I can currently do with it. There may still be possibilities, but I cannot necessarily identify or implement them yet.

I would like to return to the concepts I have saved. I cannot assume that every one will become a finished project, or that I will always know the next step. Still, keeping them gives me the opportunity to revisit them with more knowledge or more time. The fact that I cannot develop something now does not make me want to lose it.

Having ideas is always a blessing

Even if I suddenly had unlimited time and enough knowledge to build anything, I would struggle to name the first project I would choose. There are simply so many concepts. Removing the practical limits would leave me with the same difficult choice between all the things I want to explore.

I do not have a neat resolution to that feeling. I want more time, and I want to be able to do more with the ideas I already have. I will keep capturing them while they are fresh, storing the ambitious ones, and working through the challenges of the projects I can pursue.

But my view of having ideas is clear: it is always a blessing. I would rather have concepts of my own waiting to be developed than rely on copying what others have already made. I can be disappointed about limited time and still be grateful that there is so much I want to create.
