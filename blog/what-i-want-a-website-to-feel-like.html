What I Want a Website to Feel Like

I enjoy clean interfaces, but appearance is only part of it. I want people to feel comfortable using the things I build.

By KentaNovember 6, 20263 min read

I Usually Notice the Design First

One of the things I enjoy most about website development is working on the interface.

I like looking at what other designers are doing, noticing interesting layouts, and thinking about how an idea might fit into something I'm building.

Sometimes it's the typography. Sometimes it's the spacing. Sometimes it's a small interaction that makes the whole page feel more considered.

I often prefer clean, minimalist designs. But I don't think putting less on a screen automatically makes it better.

A page can be very simple and still leave you wondering what you're supposed to do.

What Happens After the First Impression?

Imagine opening a website that looks incredible.

The colours work well together. The animations are smooth. Everything looks like someone spent a lot of time on it.

Then you try to find something.

The menu labels aren't clear. A button takes you somewhere unexpected. You can't tell whether your changes have been saved.

I think that's where the design starts to matter in a different way.

The first impression might make someone interested, but the next few interactions decide how comfortable they feel.

Personally, I want to know what a button will do before I press it. I want to understand where I am and how to go back. If something goes wrong, I'd like an explanation that actually helps.

Those aren't particularly exciting features to show in a screenshot. But they're things I want to pay attention to in my own projects.

Minimal Doesn't Have to Mean Hidden

I think it's easy to become so focused on making a page look clean that you remove information people actually need.

An icon might look nicer than a longer label. But if someone has to guess what it means, the extra space probably isn't helping them very much.

The same applies to instructions. I don't want every screen to become a manual, but a short explanation in the right place can make a feature much easier to use.

For me, the interesting part is working out what deserves to stay.

What does someone need to see immediately? What can wait until they ask for it? What needs a clearer explanation?

I'm still learning how to make those decisions.

Different Projects Need Different Personalities

Even though I like a minimalist style, I wouldn't want every website I make to feel identical.

A game can be playful. A creative tool might invite experimentation. A page explaining a service needs to help someone understand what they're getting.

I enjoy the visual side of those choices, but I also think they affect how people approach the website.

If something is meant to encourage creativity, it should feel comfortable to explore. If an action has a lasting effect, it should be clear what you're about to change.

That's the kind of connection between design and functionality that interests me.

Something I'd Enjoy Using Myself

I don't expect to get every interface right the first time.

There will probably be things that seem obvious to me because I built them, while someone else finds them confusing. That's something I want to keep in mind.

But the feeling I'm aiming for is fairly simple.

I want someone to open something I've made and feel that they can work out how to use it.

They shouldn't need to understand the code or know what I was thinking when I designed it.

They should just be able to get started.
