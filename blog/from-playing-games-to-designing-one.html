From Playing Games to Designing One

From Scratch and early game ideas to web design and AI-assisted development — how my interest in creating things has changed over time.

By KentaNovember 9, 20267 min read

When I was younger, I wasn’t particularly the smartest kid academically. I was more of a gaming kid. Like many other children, I enjoyed video games, but what made my experience slightly different was that I eventually became more interested in creating games than actually playing them.

And strangely enough, it all started with a teacher.

Where It All Started: Scratch

I believe I was around Year 3 when one of my teachers introduced me to Scratch, a block-based programming platform that allows people to create games, animations, and interactive projects.

At the time, I had absolutely no idea how much that introduction would influence my interests.

I remember being incredibly excited about the idea that I could create something myself. My earliest projects were extremely simple. I started with something as basic as making a dog move up and down.

But eventually, those small experiments became more ambitious.

I started creating platformer games, then Pong, and eventually even a zombie shooter with endless rounds.

Looking back, none of those projects were particularly advanced compared to modern games, but to me, they were exciting because I was creating something that actually worked.

I wasn’t just following instructions anymore. I was experimenting with my own ideas.

Sometimes I genuinely wish I could meet that Year 3 teacher again, just to thank him. Something that might have seemed like an ordinary classroom activity ended up introducing me to an interest that has stayed with me for years.

Discovering That Game Development Isn’t Easy

As I got older, I continued using Scratch because, at the time, I didn’t really know much about other programming languages or development software.

Eventually, I discovered Unity and Blender.

I was excited. These were actual tools used for creating more advanced games, and I imagined all the things I could potentially build with them.

Unfortunately, reality was a little different.

I quickly discovered that even creating a simple character or object could take an incredibly long time.

I also wasn’t particularly artistic, which made working with 3D models even more challenging.

This was before AI-assisted development became widely accessible, so I couldn’t simply describe what I wanted and have a tool help me build it.

I had to figure things out myself, and the learning curve felt enormous.

Eventually, I stepped away from programming for a while, although I would occasionally return to experiment.

I was still interested in creating things. I just hadn’t found the right way to turn my imagination into something real.

From Games to Web Design

When I entered secondary school, I discovered something unexpected.

I actually enjoyed designing presentations.

It might sound unrelated to game development, but I found the process surprisingly enjoyable. I liked arranging elements, experimenting with layouts, and imagining how everything should look.

That interest eventually led me toward web design.

At first, I wasn’t really programming websites. I was using visual tools, dragging elements around and experimenting with different designs.

What interested me most wasn’t necessarily writing code. It was the ability to create something from an idea.

After another break, I eventually discovered Claude and AI-assisted development.

Suddenly, things that I had previously only imagined seemed much more achievable.

I could describe an idea, experiment with code, and gradually turn it into a working application.

That led me to create my first app, KineticForm.

To put it simply, KineticForm didn’t work out particularly well.

But it was still an important experience because it marked my return to software development.

And just to avoid confusion, KineticForm was my original project, not my current project, KineticPeak.

Playing Games With a Different Perspective

Nowadays, I still enjoy playing video games.

And surprisingly, I don’t spend most of my time analysing every mechanic or wondering how everything was programmed.

Most of the time, I just want to enjoy the game.

However, there is one thing that occasionally catches my attention: the user interface.

Sometimes I come across games with menus that are unnecessarily complicated.

And I think that’s a genuine problem.

A game could have interesting mechanics, impressive graphics, and plenty of content, but if players struggle to navigate its menus, they might lose interest before they even experience what makes the game enjoyable.

This is something I’ve also come to appreciate through web design.

An interface shouldn’t make people work harder than necessary.

It should make the experience easier.

What Actually Makes a Game Fun?

If someone asked me what makes a video game enjoyable, my answer would be fairly simple.

Creativity, simplicity, and a reasonable level of difficulty.

I don’t think a game needs to be incredibly complicated to be entertaining.

In fact, sometimes making something unnecessarily difficult can take away from the experience.

I enjoy games that offer creative ideas while remaining accessible enough for players to have fun without constantly struggling.

That doesn’t mean games shouldn’t be challenging. I just don’t think difficulty should become the entire experience.

If I had the opportunity to create my dream game without worrying about technical limitations, I would probably make a first-person shooter.

But rather than focusing entirely on traditional modes like 1v1 matches, I’d want to experiment with different, less conventional game modes.

I think there’s plenty of room for creativity within familiar genres.

From Playing to Creating

When I look back at my journey, it’s interesting how my interests have changed.

I started as a kid experimenting with Scratch, moved toward Unity and Blender, stepped away from programming, discovered visual design, and eventually returned to software development through AI-assisted tools.

It hasn’t been a straightforward journey.

There have been plenty of moments when something felt too complicated or when a project simply didn’t work out.

But I don’t think those experiences were wasted.

Even though my focus has shifted toward websites and software, my original interest in creating games hasn’t completely disappeared.

And if I could give my younger self one piece of advice, it would be this:

Keep trying, but don’t commit so much that you burn yourself out.

I think it’s important to experiment, explore different interests, and enjoy the process of creating things without turning every project into an obligation.

After all, the reason I started making games in the first place wasn’t because I wanted to achieve something impressive.

It was because I thought creating something of my own would be fun.

And I think that’s still a pretty good reason to keep creating.
